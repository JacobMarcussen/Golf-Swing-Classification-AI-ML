# P4 — Top — Form

## What this position is

The Top position (P4) is the transition point between the backswing and the downswing — the brief moment where the club reaches its highest point and changes direction. It is not necessarily a pause; in most efficient swings the lower body has already begun to initiate the downswing before the club reaches P4. The position is defined by the club shaft reaching its maximum elevation relative to the swing plane, with the lead arm typically between horizontal and vertical depending on the player's flexibility and style.

P4 is arguably the most studied checkpoint in golf instruction because it is the culmination of everything built in P1 through P3. The quality of P4 determines the club's initial path in the downswing (P5) and has a direct causal relationship with impact (P6).

## Body posture

At P4, the shoulders have typically rotated 80-100° from their address position, while the hips have rotated 35-55°. The difference — the X-factor — represents the maximum coil in the swing and is a key predictor of clubhead speed. Elite players often achieve X-factor values of 45-55°, though this varies considerably by age, flexibility, and style.

Spine angle from vertical is maintained close to the address position of 30-45°. A very common fault at the top is a reverse spine angle — where the torso tilts toward the target rather than away from it — which is both a power leak and a lower back injury risk. Weight has shifted predominantly onto the trail side, typically 70-80% on the trail foot. The trail knee remains in flex (15-25°), bracing against the hip rotation. The lead heel may rise slightly off the ground depending on flexibility, though many modern players keep the lead foot flat for better stability.

## Arm and club position

The lead arm should be approximately parallel to the ground or slightly above (0-30° above horizontal is the typical pro range). A lead arm significantly above horizontal often indicates overswinging; significantly below indicates a restricted backswing. The lead elbow is ideally near-straight, 155-180°, though some players play with a slightly bent lead elbow at the top — this is a style variation rather than an absolute fault.

The trail arm is folded with the elbow pointing roughly downward (not flying outward), typically at 80-100°. The wrist hinge is fully established: the lead wrist is flat or very slightly bowed (0-10° of dorsiflexion is neutral to slightly strong), and the club shaft is ideally parallel to the ground and pointing toward the target — this is the classic "on-plane at the top" reference. The clubface should be roughly parallel to the lead forearm and the lead wrist.

## Typical pro ranges

- Spine angle from vertical: 30-45° (maintained from address)
- Hip rotation from address: 35-55°
- Shoulder rotation from address: 80-100°
- X-factor (shoulder-hip differential): 35-55°
- Lead arm above horizontal: 0-30°
- Lead arm angle (elbow): 155-180°
- Trail arm angle (elbow): 80-100°
- Lead wrist flexion/extension: -10° to +10° (flat to slightly bowed)
- Shaft angle from vertical: 80-100° (roughly parallel to ground for a driver)
- Weight distribution: 70-80% trail / 20-30% lead

## Why it matters

The position at the top is the reset point for the entire downswing. A club that is across the line (pointing right of target for a righty) tends to cause an inside-out downswing or a block. A club that is laid off (pointing left of target) tends to cause an over-the-top move. The lead wrist angle at the top directly determines whether the face arrives open, closed, or square at impact. Coaches often prioritize achieving a sound P4 because correcting it can simultaneously resolve multiple impact faults downstream.

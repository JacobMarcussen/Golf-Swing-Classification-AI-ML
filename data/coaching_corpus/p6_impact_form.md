# P6 — Impact — Form

## What this position is

Impact (P6) is the moment the clubface contacts the ball. It is the only moment in the swing that actually determines ball flight — everything before it is in service of this position, and everything after it is a consequence. Because the club is moving at speeds of 80-120+ mph at this point, impact is not a position the golfer consciously creates; it is the result of all the positions and sequences that preceded it. Nonetheless, understanding what a good impact position looks like is essential for diagnosing and correcting faults.

Despite being a product of the preceding swing, impact does not look like address. The hips are open to the target, the hands are ahead of the ball, the trail heel is rising, and the spine has lateral tilt — all of these are hallmarks of a powerful, efficient delivery.

## Body posture

At impact, the hips are open 35-50° relative to address (toward the target) for a driver, and slightly less for short irons. The shoulders are considerably less open — typically 15-25° open — preserving the kinematic chain that was built during the downswing. The torso has a noticeable lateral tilt away from the target (the trail shoulder is lower than the lead shoulder for a driver), typically 5-10°, a consequence of the spine's tilt at address being maintained through impact.

Spine angle from vertical is close to address (30-45°), though early extension faults show a reduced spine angle here. Weight has shifted heavily onto the lead side — 80-95% on the lead foot at impact — and the trail heel is usually elevated 1-5 cm off the ground as the body's momentum transfers forward. The lead knee has largely straightened to a stable post position; the trail knee continues driving inward and toward the target line.

## Arm and club position

One of the most important features of a good impact position is forward shaft lean: the hands are ahead of the clubhead, with the shaft leaning toward the target by typically 4-10° from vertical. This forward lean compresses the ball against the face, reduces loft, and is the hallmark of a ball-striking professional versus an amateur who has the shaft vertical or leaning backward at impact.

The lead arm is approximately straight and in line with the shaft (170-180° at the lead elbow), forming the "single lever" that transmits force to the ball. The trail arm has extended significantly from its P5 position but is not yet fully straight — trail elbow angle is typically 130-160° at impact. The lead wrist is flat or slightly bowed (lead wrist flexion 0-10°), matching the forward shaft lean. The clubface should be square to the swing path within the margin required for the intended shot shape.

## Typical pro ranges

- Spine angle from vertical: 30-45° (maintained from address)
- Spine lateral tilt (away from target): 5-10° for driver
- Hip rotation (open): 35-50° from address
- Shoulder rotation (open): 15-25° from address
- Lead arm angle (elbow): 170-180°
- Trail arm angle (elbow): 130-160°
- Forward shaft lean: 4-10° toward target from vertical
- Lead wrist flexion: 0-10° (flat to slightly bowed)
- Weight distribution: 80-95% lead / 5-20% trail
- Trail heel rise: 1-5 cm

## Why it matters

Impact is the final arbitrator of ball flight. A square clubface with the correct path and angle of attack produces the desired shot; any deviation propagates directly into the ball's initial direction and spin axis. Although impact cannot be directly trained in isolation (it happens too fast), understanding what a good impact position looks like allows coaches to identify which upstream positions need correction to produce a better impact. Forward shaft lean, lead-side weight, and a square face are the three most actionable targets for impact improvement.

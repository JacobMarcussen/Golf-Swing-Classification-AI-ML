# P3 — Mid-backswing — Common Faults and Fixes

## Over-the-top swing plane (too steep / outside)

**What it looks like:** The club shaft at P3 points well above the trail shoulder line when viewed from behind, indicating a steep, outside-in swing plane. The lead arm is often higher than horizontal, and the club head may have moved outside the hands.

**Detection signal:** `shaft proxy angle` showing steep orientation above the ideal plane line; `left_shoulder` elevated significantly; `shoulder_line_from_horizontal` more tilted than expected for this stage of the swing.

**Why it happens:** A common cause is using the arms to lift the club rather than rotating the body. Golfers who take the club outside at P2 and then lift tend to produce this fault. It often causes an over-the-top downswing path and a pull or slice.

**Fix:** Focus on rotating the torso rather than lifting the arms. Drill: make half-swings where the feeling is that the hands stay low and the shoulders do the lifting. A towel tucked under the trail armpit that doesn't fall out during the backswing helps keep the trail arm connected and prevents lifting.

## Flat swing plane (too shallow / inside)

**What it looks like:** At P3, the shaft points well below the ideal plane — typically below the trail shoulder, sometimes almost horizontal. The club has stayed too far inside and too low.

**Detection signal:** `shaft proxy angle` showing a shallow, inside orientation; `right_elbow` may be excessively tucked behind the body (flying elbow in reverse); `shoulder_line_from_horizontal` appearing flat rather than tilted.

**Why it happens:** Usually traces back to an inside takeaway at P2. The golfer may also be trying to sweep the ball or avoid hitting across it, and consciously flattens the plane.

**Fix:** Allow the trail elbow to fold while the lead arm rises with the shoulder turn. Drill: make backswings with a glove held under the lead armpit; it should fall out by P3 naturally as the shoulder turn elevates the lead arm.

## Collapsed lead arm (chicken wing backswing)

**What it looks like:** The lead elbow bends significantly through the mid-backswing — often folding to 130° or less — causing a loss of width in the swing arc. The club is typically too close to the body and the swing arc is narrow.

**Detection signal:** `left_elbow` angle substantially below 150°; lead arm arc appearing compact in face-on view; `left_shoulder` elevation inconsistent with expected arm position.

**Why it happens:** Insufficient shoulder turn causes the body to compensate by bending the lead arm to complete the backswing. Also common in players with poor shoulder flexibility or who try to keep the lead elbow "soft."

**Fix:** Prioritize shoulder turn over arm lift. The lead arm stays extended as a consequence of good rotation, not conscious effort. Drill: practice swinging with a training aid (like a pool noodle) slid over the lead arm; the restriction prevents folding.

## Insufficient wrist hinge (casting setup)

**What it looks like:** At P3, the club shaft is still largely in line with the lead arm rather than cocked upward. The wrists have not hinged, leaving the club in a low, extended position. This preloads a casting motion in the downswing.

**Detection signal:** `shaft proxy angle` showing the club nearly in line with the lead arm at P3, where a 70-100° wrist hinge angle is expected; `left_wrist` angle remaining near 180° rather than reducing toward 90°.

**Why it happens:** Some golfers consciously avoid wrist action out of fear of inconsistency. Others were taught a one-lever swing and never learned to hinge. Tight forearm and wrist muscles can also limit hinge.

**Fix:** Allow natural wrist hinge — it should occur as a response to the weight of the club and centrifugal force rather than as a conscious flick. Drill: make practice swings stopping at P3 and checking that the club shaft points roughly at the sky (perpendicular to the lead arm).

## Hip sway (lateral drift away from target)

**What it looks like:** Rather than rotating, the hips slide laterally away from the target during the backswing. The trail hip moves outside the trail foot, the spine tilts toward the target (reverse spine), and the weight distribution is often poorly managed.

**Detection signal:** `right_hip` and `left_hip` positions showing lateral displacement rather than rotation; `hip_line_from_horizontal` appearing more tilted than expected; `spine_angle_from_vertical` decreasing unexpectedly or the torso tilting toward the target.

**Why it happens:** The golfer confuses weight shift with rotation. Moving laterally feels like loading the trail side. Sometimes caused by a wide stance that restricts hip rotation.

**Fix:** Rotate the right hip (for a right-handed golfer) in a barrel — feel the hip turn behind rather than slide to the right. Drill: make backswings with the trail foot against a wall; the hip cannot sway and must rotate instead.
